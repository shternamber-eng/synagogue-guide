Contemporary Synagogue
Architects & Designers

The visionaries reshaping Jewish sacred space. Meet the leading contemporary architects and designers creating inspiring, functional, and spiritually resonant synagogue buildings across America and the world.

The synagogue building is a sacred vessel. Unlike many religious structures, it holds multiple purposes simultaneously: a place for communal prayer, a classroom for learning, a gathering space for celebration and mourning, and a repository of the community's history and identity. Designing such a space requires not just architectural skill, but a deep understanding of Jewish practice, liturgy, and community needs.

In recent decades, a new generation of architects and designers has emerged — professionals who combine technical expertise with sensitivity to Jewish tradition and modern community values. These visionaries are reshaping the landscape of Jewish sacred space across America and the world.

The Challenge of Synagogue Design

Contemporary synagogue design faces unique challenges. Unlike a church, where the congregation faces a single focal point (the altar), synagogues must accommodate a rabbi on the bimah (platform), a hazzan (cantor) who may occupy a different location, a Torah ark that is the true spiritual center, and seating that allows for dynamic community participation. The design must balance tradition with accessibility, intimacy with grandeur, and preservation with innovation.

Moreover, many American synagogues today are renovating or reimagining older buildings — structures built a century ago that no longer serve contemporary communities' needs. This requires architects who understand both historic preservation and modern accessibility, who can honor the past while creating spaces that feel alive and welcoming to 21st-century congregations.

Leading Contemporary Synagogue Architects

Esther Sperber — Studio ST Architects

Based in New York City, Esther Sperber's Studio ST Architects has become a leading voice in contemporary synagogue design. Sperber's work is characterized by thoughtful integration of Jewish tradition with modern architectural language. Her designs often feature clean lines, natural materials, and careful attention to how light and space create spiritual experiences.

What distinguishes Sperber's approach is her deep engagement with Jewish communities. Before a single design line is drawn, she spends extensive time understanding the congregation's history, liturgical practices, and aspirations for the future. This community-centered methodology results in buildings that feel authentically owned by their congregations while pushing the boundaries of contemporary sacred architecture.

Stanley Saitowitz — Natoma Architects

San Francisco-based Stanley Saitowitz brings a distinctive modernist sensibility to synagogue design. Known for bold geometric forms and innovative use of materials, Saitowitz creates spaces that are both visually striking and functionally excellent. His work often incorporates natural light, sustainable materials, and thoughtful integration with the surrounding landscape.

Saitowitz's synagogue projects demonstrate how contemporary architecture can honor Jewish tradition while embracing modern aesthetics. His designs frequently feature dynamic spatial relationships that enhance the liturgical experience, with careful attention to acoustics, sightlines, and the flow of movement through sacred space.

David Adjaye — Adjaye Associates

Internationally renowned architect David Adjaye has designed several significant Jewish institutional spaces. Known for his work on the National Museum of African American History and Culture in Washington, D.C., Adjaye brings a global perspective to sacred architecture. His synagogue designs are marked by thoughtful materiality, cultural sensitivity, and innovative spatial solutions.

Adjaye's approach emphasizes the relationship between building and community, often incorporating elements that reflect the congregation's cultural heritage while creating spaces that feel contemporary and welcoming. His work demonstrates how international architectural language can serve Jewish sacred purposes.

Manuel Herz — Basel, Switzerland

Swiss architect Manuel Herz has emerged as a significant figure in contemporary synagogue design, particularly in Europe and North America. Herz's work is characterized by minimalist aesthetics, careful attention to proportion, and the integration of natural elements. His designs often feature clean geometries, sustainable materials, and thoughtful relationships between interior and exterior spaces.

Herz brings a European sensibility to synagogue architecture, emphasizing simplicity and functionality while maintaining spiritual resonance. His projects often incorporate innovative lighting solutions and flexible spaces that can adapt to changing community needs.

Julian de Metz — dMFK Architects

London-based Julian de Metz of dMFK Architects has designed several notable synagogue projects in the UK and internationally. De Metz's work combines traditional architectural principles with contemporary innovation, often featuring thoughtful restoration of historic buildings alongside bold new additions.

His approach emphasizes the continuity of Jewish architectural tradition while embracing modern functionality. De Metz's designs often incorporate natural light, sustainable materials, and careful attention to how spaces facilitate Jewish ritual and community life.

Field Architecture

Silicon Valley-based Field Architecture brings a tech-forward sensibility to synagogue design. Known for innovative use of materials and technology, Field creates spaces that are both technologically advanced and spiritually resonant. Their work often incorporates smart building systems, flexible programming spaces, and designs that reflect the diverse, multicultural nature of contemporary Jewish communities.

Field Architecture's synagogue projects demonstrate how cutting-edge design can serve traditional Jewish purposes, with careful attention to acoustics, lighting, and the integration of digital elements that enhance rather than distract from the sacred experience.

Building Studio Architects

New York-based Building Studio Architects specializes in thoughtful, community-driven synagogue design. Their work is characterized by careful research into each congregation's unique needs and history, resulting in buildings that feel both contemporary and deeply rooted in Jewish tradition.

Building Studio's approach emphasizes collaboration with rabbis, community leaders, and congregants throughout the design process. Their projects often feature innovative spatial solutions, sustainable design principles, and careful attention to accessibility and inclusivity.

Key Principles in Contemporary Synagogue Design

Community Participation

The best contemporary synagogue architects involve their clients deeply in the design process. This means conducting extensive interviews, holding community charrettes, and creating design processes that allow congregants to feel ownership of the final product.

Flexibility

Modern congregations are smaller and more diverse than many historical synagogues. The design must accommodate multiple uses: intimate weekday services, larger Friday night gatherings, High Holiday crowds, educational programming, social events. This requires flexible seating, movable elements, and spaces that can be configured in multiple ways.

Natural Light

Almost universally, contemporary synagogue designers emphasize natural light. Light is essential to Jewish practice (the eternal light above the ark, the Shabbat candles, the Yahrzeit lights), and contemporary designers use sophisticated glazing, skylights, and carefully controlled apertures to bring natural light into sacred spaces in ways that feel intentional and spiritually significant.

Materials That Age

There is a trend toward materials that visibly age — brick that weathers, wood that develops patina, copper that oxidizes. This reflects a theological principle: the building itself becomes a record of time, of community history, of the generations who have gathered within its walls.

Accessibility and Inclusion

Contemporary designers prioritize universal design principles. Ramps, elevators, accessible restrooms, and assistive listening systems are not afterthoughts but integral to the design. Similarly, designers think carefully about how to include people with different abilities in ritual participation — how someone using a wheelchair can approach the Torah, how someone who is deaf can participate in music and prayer.

Case Studies: Notable Recent Projects

Historic Sanctuary Renovations

Many American synagogues occupy buildings constructed in the early-to-mid 20th century. Some of these structures are architecturally significant; nearly all require updating for modern accessibility and systems (HVAC, electrical, plumbing). The challenge for architects is to preserve what is sacred and historic while making the space functional for contemporary congregations.

Successful renovations often involve careful restoration of original architectural elements — ornamental plasterwork, stained glass, carved arks — combined with tactful insertion of new systems. A well-executed renovation feels seamless; you would not know that the building's heating system was completely replaced or that an elevator was invisibly inserted to improve accessibility.

New-Build Sacred Spaces

Occasionally, a synagogue builds entirely new. These projects offer opportunities to create from scratch — spaces that reflect contemporary Jewish theology, community demographics, and practice. New-build projects tend to feature more flexible spaces, better integration with educational and social programming areas, and more efficient systems.

Urban Sanctuaries in Shared Buildings

Some contemporary congregations worship in multi-use facilities or shared spaces within larger buildings. This presents unique design challenges: how to create a sense of sanctuary and separation within a secular building, how to make the space feel Jewish and welcoming despite constraints, how to maintain acoustic integrity while serving multiple purposes.

The Future of Synagogue Architecture

Sustainability

An emerging principle in contemporary synagogue design is environmental sustainability. Buildings that are energy-efficient, use sustainable materials, and minimize environmental impact reflect Jewish values of stewardship (bal tashchit — do not waste). Some progressive synagogues are targeting net-zero energy performance, green building certifications, and carbon-neutral operations.

Intergenerational Design

Contemporary architects are increasingly thinking about how to create spaces that serve multiple generations. This might mean designing worship spaces that work for traditional liturgical practices while also creating contemporary prayer spaces, or ensuring that educational areas appeal to children, teenagers, and adults.

Digital Integration

The pandemic accelerated the integration of technology into synagogue design. Many congregations now livestream services, offer hybrid participation, and use digital displays for announcements and educational content. This requires architects to think about acoustics, sightlines, and infrastructure for technology in ways that previous generations did not.

Community-Centered Programming Spaces

As American Jewry becomes more diverse, synagogues are expanding their role as community institutions. This means designing flexible programming spaces that can host Hebrew school, social action initiatives, interfaith gatherings, cultural events, and more. The synagogue building itself becomes a statement: this is not just a sanctuary, but a hub of Jewish communal life.

Engaging an Architect for Your Congregation

If your congregation is considering renovation, expansion, or new construction, the selection of the right architect is critical. Consider the following:

  • Experience with Jewish institutional design: Does the firm have a track record of synagogue projects? Do they understand Jewish liturgy and community practice?
  • References and site visits: Ask for references from previous synagogue clients and, if possible, visit completed projects to experience them firsthand.
  • Community engagement: How does the firm involve congregants in the design process? Do they conduct community meetings and incorporate feedback?
  • Financial realism: Does the architect understand your budget constraints and have experience delivering projects on budget?
  • Sustainability commitment: Is the firm knowledgeable about green building practices and can they help your congregation achieve sustainability goals?
  • Accessibility expertise: Does the firm prioritize universal design and understand accessibility requirements for religious facilities?

The Theology of Sacred Space

Ultimately, synagogue architecture is not merely practical. It is theological. The way a building is designed communicates messages about God, community, prayer, and Jewish identity.

When natural light fills a sanctuary, it evokes the divine. When a building is designed with everyone's accessibility in mind, it embodies the Jewish principle of kavod habriyot — the inherent dignity of every person. When worship and education spaces are integrated, the design declares that learning and prayer are inseparable. When a building is made of durable materials that will outlast any single generation, it affirms faith in the continuity of Jewish community.

The leading contemporary synagogue architects understand this. They are not simply designing buildings. They are creating containers for Jewish life — spaces where generations to come will celebrate births and welcome new members, where mourners will find community, where children will first encounter Jewish tradition, where elderly members will find welcome and dignity, and where the Jewish people will continue to speak with God in the languages and forms of their own time.
